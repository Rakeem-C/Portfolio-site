The most expensive mistakes I’ve seen weren’t sloppy. They were careful, well-executed solutions to the wrong problem.

That happens because symptoms are persuasive. They show up first, they’re easy to see, and they come with a story attached. The cause is usually quieter and one level down.

## The method

**1. Measure before you explain.** Write down what the data actually says before forming a theory. Stories are cheap. Numbers push back.

**2. Reduce to what must be true.** Strip away assumptions one at a time. What’s left is the part of the problem you can reason about from first principles.

**3. Attack from every angle.** I run through six on purpose:

- **Data:** What do the numbers say, and what would they say if I were wrong?
- **Incentives:** Who benefits if this stays broken?
- **Workflow:** Where does the work really happen, as opposed to where the diagram says it happens?
- **Tools:** Is the tool failing, or is it being used for the wrong job?
- **People:** What do they know, and what are they afraid of?
- **Physics:** What constraints can’t change, no matter how good the idea is?

If an explanation only survives from one of these directions, it’s probably a symptom.

**4. Prove it.** A fix isn’t finished when it ships. It’s finished when the evidence shows the failure can’t quietly come back.

## Why this matters for AI

AI makes it cheaper than ever to produce a confident answer. That makes this habit more important, not less. The faster you can generate solutions, the more it costs to aim them at the wrong problem.
